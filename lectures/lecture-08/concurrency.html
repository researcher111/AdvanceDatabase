Concurrency: Locks, Isolation & MVCC

Two transactions, one row, and every way that can go wrong, named, priced, and prevented.
Daniel G. Graham · UVA School of Data Science

The gold standard is serializability: the result equals some serial order. Everything else on today's menu is a named, priced deviation from it.

War story · the lost update that closed a bank, 2014

The anomaly zoo is not academic. In 2014 the Bitcoin exchange Flexcoin was destroyed by the first row of that table: attackers fired thousands of simultaneous transfers between accounts they controlled, and the transfer code did read-balance → check → write-balance with no isolation3. Concurrent reads saw the same balance, the debits overwrote each other (a textbook lost update), and the attackers withdrew about 896 BTC the exchange didn't have3. Flexcoin announced its closure within days. Two cashiers serving one account is that exploit with smaller numbers; the fix is today's entire lecture.

Two-phase locking

The classic prevention: make conflicting access impossible by fencing rows with locks. Every serious engine shipped it for decades; yours ships it Thursday in 40 lines.

S lock (shared / read):     many holders welcome, readers coexist
X lock (exclusive / write): one holder, no readers, no exceptions

conflict table:        S      X
                 S    ok    WAIT
                 X   WAIT   WAIT        ← every anomaly dies in these three WAITs4

The protocol

RuleStatementWhy
Lock before touchingS (shared) lock to read, many holders; X (exclusive) to write, one holder, no readerswrite-write and read-write overlaps become impossible
Two phasesacquire locks growing, never after the first release5the growing/shrinking split is what makes schedules provably serializable
Strict varianthold everything until commit/rollbackalso kills dirty reads; what your lab (and most engines) implement4

The bill: waiting, and worse

Waitinga conflicting lock means someone sits idle: the report blocks the update blocks the report. Throughput dies of politeness.
Deadlocktx1 holds A wants B; tx2 holds B wants A, forever. Engines detect the cycle and shoot one participant (your app must retry)6.
microdb's dodgesingle-threaded, so waiting would be waiting on nobody: conflicts raise LockAbortError immediately. Same protocol, honest scale.
The itchmost conflicts involve a reader, and readers don't change anything. Must a report really block a deposit? Hold that thought two sections.

Isolation levels

The menu, explorable

four levels, three anomalies · click a level, watch what it buys

Each card is one anomaly's two-transaction story. Climbing the ladder turns warm cards (can happen) green (prevented), and raises the price at the bottom. Every level is a different point on the safety-for-throughput trade, not a different degree of broken.

Climb from bottom to top and watch the order the anomalies die in; that order IS the standard's definition of the levels7.

Set it per transaction: SET TRANSACTION ISOLATION LEVEL SERIALIZABLE8. The gray zone at REPEATABLE READ is why the Berenson paper in the citations exists: the ANSI names predate the implementations, and write skew slips between them (FAQ)2.

MVCC: readers don't block

How Postgres actually does it

PieceMechanismYou already built the bones
VersionsUPDATE = insert a new row version; the old one stays, stamped (xmin, xmax) with creator/deleter tx ids9Lab 3: inserts into free slots; tombstones linger
Snapshotseach tx knows which tx ids were committed at its start; it sees exactly those versions10 
Visibility checkper row: xmin committed-before-me AND xmax not, a two-field test, no locks11a Predicate, really (Lab 4)
The trashVACUUM reclaims versions no living snapshot can see12Lab 3's tombstone reuse, industrialized

Readers never block writers; writers never block readers. Writers still block writers (locks live on)6, and long-lived snapshots make vacuum's job harder: MVCC moved the cost, it didn't erase it13. No free level, ever.

Walk a version chain

One account, three updates, four possible readers. The two-field visibility test from the table above, run live; drag the slider to move the reader's snapshot through time:

History: tx 100 inserted the row at 120, tx 103 updated it to 70, tx 107 updated it to 50. Each version's test: visible if xmin committed before my snapshot AND xmax was not. Green = the version this reader sees; every other reader, concurrently, sees its own.

No locks appear anywhere in this test; it's pure arithmetic on two stamps, which is why a 10-minute report and a write flood coexist. The versions the slider leaves behind are VACUUM's job; a reader parked at the leftmost snapshot is exactly the "long-lived snapshot" that makes vacuum's job harder.

FAQ

What exactly is write skew? The table teased it.

The on-call rule: at least one doctor rostered. Two doctors each run "if the OTHER is on call, I can leave": under snapshot isolation both reads see the old roster, both commit, zero doctors remain11. No row was written by both, so no write conflict fired. Only true serializability (Postgres's SSI) catches it; the reason SERIALIZABLE still exists as a level above snapshot.

Why doesn't microdb's Lab 7 implement MVCC?

Locks are 40 lines and teach the protocol; MVCC is version chains, snapshot bookkeeping, and vacuum, a semester garnish, not a Thursday. You now know both designs and, more importantly, the trade they're making. Sciore's SimpleDB stops at locks too, though his chapter 5 does sketch multiversion locking15.

How often do real deadlocks happen?

Constantly, at scale: check pg_stat_database.deadlocks14 on any busy system. The standard defenses are ordering (all code touches accounts in id order, no cycles possible) and retry loops6. "Transactions may be shot; write retryable code" is professional-grade advice you now understand.

Does the buffer pool's pin count relate to these locks?

Different layer, different lifetime: a pin protects a frame for microseconds (week 2, and the latch world of Lab 3's FAQ); a lock protects a row/block's meaning for the transaction's whole life. Same shaped API, three orders of magnitude apart in duration; confusing them is a classic systems interview trap you're now immune to.
