RAG as a Systems Problem

The model has never seen your documents and cannot hold them all in its prompt, so how does "chat with your documents" work at all?
Daniel G. Graham · UVA School of Data Science

Ask a language model about your own documents and it has nothing to answer from. It answers from parametric knowledge, whatever its training run baked into the weights1; your documents aren't in there, and its context window is too small to hold them all. Retrieval-augmented generation is the standard fix, and the central claim of this lecture is that the "retrieval" half is not an AI technique. It is a database: chunked records, an embedding column, a vector index, a top-k query, and an evaluation metric. You built every one of those pieces already. Thursday you wire them into a working RAG pipeline; your project is the same wiring at real scale.

Companion reading: Lewis et al., Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks (the paper that named it)1 · Liu et al., Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts · pgvector + Postgres full-text: hybrid search in one query2.

Dotted-underlined terms like this one are systems jargon; hover or tap for a plain-language explainer that opens right below the sentence you're reading.

Today's plan

Quiz 9 first (vector search, 10 min), then the pipeline your project is building.

Your project's corpus is not in any model's weights, and it will not fit in any model's prompt. Nothing about the model fixes that. What fixes it is a database, and you have already built most of it.

Today's claim: RAG quality is mostly retrieval quality, and retrieval quality is measurable, with a hand-labeled eval set, not by reading a few answers and forming an impression.

Teaching a model your data is a database workload

Three ways to teach a model your data

ApproachPer updatePer questionWhere it breaks
Fine-tune the weightsa full training run, repeated every time the documents changecheap: the question alone, with no extra tokens attachedthe weights are stale the moment a document changes, and the model recalls specific facts from them unreliably3. It also cannot cite: the answer comes from weights, not from a document it can point to
Stuff the context with everythingfree: just send the new text next timeevery document is billed as prompt tokens on every questiona 50k-document corpus does not fit in any context window, and what does fit gets skimmed: models attend poorly to evidence buried in the middle of a long prompt, the "lost in the middle" effect measured later in this lecture
Retrieve, then generatere-embed only the changed document and update the indexone top-k query, plus the k retrieved chunks sent as prompt tokensthe answer can only be as good as the chunks retrieved: if the right chunk does not come back, the model never sees it. That is why this lecture measures retrieval

Retrieval turns the problem into a database workload

Retrieval wins on update cost, question cost, and citability, by turning the problem into a database workload: keep documents in a store, index them for similarity, query per question. Fresh data means updating an index, not retraining a model.

Chunking is record design, not text splitting



Whole document, paragraph, or sentence




  
ChoiceRetrieval precisionContext deliveredFailure mode

  
    
whole documentpoor: the whole document collapses into one vector that averages every topic it covers4huge, and most of it has nothing to do with the questionan averaged vector is a little similar to every question and very similar to none, so the document is rarely the top hit for anything

    
paragraph / ~100–300 wordsgood: one topic per vector, so a question about that topic lands close to ita self-contained claim, with enough text to answer fromfew; this is the size most production systems settle on5

    
sentencesharp when the wording matches, but a single sentence has so few words that a small change in phrasing misses ittoo little to answer from: a sentence usually depends on the ones around itthe retrieved sentence says "it does this" and the "it" was defined two sentences earlier, in a chunk that did not come back

  




Overlap and metadata are record design too




  
Overlaprepeat ~10–15% of the text at chunk boundaries, so a claim split across chunks survives whole in one of them6.

  
Metadatastore the doc id, title, and position with each chunk; Thursday's chunks carry doc and title precisely so answers can cite.

  
Same decisionSciore would call these production refinements a layout decision; LangChain calls them a text splitter7. Either way it is record design: deciding what one stored chunk carries.

Retrieval quality is measurable: two numbers, twelve questions



Two numbers from one hand-labeled set





The eval set is 12 pairs of (question, relevant doc ids), labeled by hand, once. Two numbers
come out of it: hit@k and MRR, the mean reciprocal rank8:

\[ \text{hit@}k \;=\; \frac{\#\{\text{questions with a relevant doc in the top } k\}}{\#\text{questions}} \qquad \text{(did it come back?)} \]
\[ \text{MRR} \;=\; \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{q \in Q} \frac{1}{\text{rank}_q} \qquad \text{(how high?)} \]

where rankq is the position of the first relevant doc: rank 1 scores 1.0, rank 2 scores
0.5, rank 3 scores 0.33, and a doc that never appears scores 0.





One config line moved hit@3 from 0.42 to 0.92



Thursday's embedder is hashed bag-of-words: each word in the text is run through a
hash function that picks one of DIM slots in the vector, and that slot is incremented, so the width DIM is a dial.
Shrink it and unrelated words collide onto shared dimensions, so two texts that share no words start to
look similar, and the eval set catches the damage without anyone reading an answer9:

RAG fails the way databases fail, starting with vocabulary



The synonym wall, measured



Measured on the widget's corpus above: same information need,
two phrasings, DIM=2048:



"Why can't the OS page cache replace the database's own cache?"
      → bufferpool (0.25) ✓     wal (0.13)      catalog (0.12)

"What keeps the most popular pages sitting in memory?"
      → wal (0.10) ✗            selectivity (0.09)      blocks (0.09)     [bufferpool: absent]




  
The synonym walltwo phrasings of the same question share no words, so an embedder that matches words cannot tell they mean the same thing.

  
Why it happensbag-of-words matches words, not meaning: "popular pages in memory" shares no content words with the buffer-pool document, so it retrieves noise at scores barely above zero.

  
The fixa learned embedding model maps both phrasings near the same point. That upgrade is exactly one function swap in Thursday's code, and it is the swap your project makes.





Retrieved is not read: two ranking failures




  
Lost in the middlemodels attend best to the start and end of a long context; retrieved-but-buried evidence gets skimmed (Liu et al. above measured the U-shape)10. Mitigation: retrieve less, order deliberately, put the best chunk first.11

  
Right doc, wrong rankthe relevant chunk is retrieved but lands third in a list of five, so hit@3 counts the question as a success even though the model reads two weaker chunks first. MRR is the metric that notices, because rank 3 scores 0.33 instead of 1.0. The fix belongs to the assembly stage: order the retrieved chunks so the best-scoring one comes first.





Staleness, drift, and overconfidence are database failures too




  
Stale indexdocuments changed; vectors didn't. The index answers from the past with full confidence, the RAG version of the cache-invalidation problem, solved the same way: re-embed on write, version the index.

  
Confident grounding failureretrieval returns weak chunks; the model answers anyway, citing them. Defenses: score thresholds ("if the best cosine is 0.09, say so"), and prompts that permit "the sources don't answer this."

  
Embedding driftchange the embedding model and every stored vector becomes meaningless, because vectors from two different models do not live in the same space and distances between them say nothing (last week's lesson, now as an operations problem). Treat the embedding model's version like a schema version: record which model produced each vector, and re-embed the whole corpus to migrate.





Fabricated citations have already reached a courtroom




  War story · Mata v. Avianca, 2023
  
One of these failures has already reached a courtroom. In 2023, lawyers in a routine
  New York airline suit filed a brief citing several judicial opinions that did not exist12;
  ChatGPT had generated them, complete with plausible names, docket numbers, and quotes12, and
  when asked to verify, it cheerfully vouched for its own inventions12. A federal judge
  sanctioned the lawyers, and "check whether the case is real" entered legal practice
  overnight. Read it as an architecture lesson: an LLM answering from
  parametric memory fabricates fluently, and only a retrieval layer over a real,
  citable corpus (with the citations carried through, like your [doc_id]
  tags) turns "sounds right" into "is checkably there."






Production upgrades are access-path ideas you already know



The production retrieval stack



Three upgrades separate Thursday's 200 lines from a production
retrieval stack, each one a database idea you can now name:




  
UpgradeWhat it isDatabase idea underneath

  
    
Hybrid searchrun BM25 keyword search AND vector search, merge the ranked lists13two indexes over the same table, and the query uses both access paths and unions the results. Keyword search finds exact tokens (an error code, a function name) that the synonym wall hides from vectors; vector search finds paraphrases that share no exact words. Each covers the other's misses

    
Rerankingretrieve a generous top-50 cheaply, then let a reranker model re-order and keep 514a two-phase query plan: run the cheap, selective step (the vector index) first, then run the expensive operator (the reranker) only on the few rows that survive. It is the same cost logic as predicate pushdown, applied to a model instead of a predicate

    
Metadata filteringrestrict the similarity search to chunks whose stored metadata matches a predicate, for example "top-k most similar AND team = 'infra' AND date > 2025"a predicate combined with an index scan, and the reason vectors-inside-your-database (pgvector, DuckDB VSS) beats a detached vector service for filtered workloads15

FAQ



Context windows keep growing: will long context kill RAG?


Long context raises the ceiling on how much you can send,
not on whether sending everything is wise: you pay per token per question, latency grows11, and
"lost in the middle" means a model can hold a long context without attending to all of it equally. Retrieval is the per-question cost
optimizer; the bigger the corpus, the more it saves. Windows grow, but corpora
grow faster, so retrieval stays necessary.



Why not fine-tune on our documents instead?


Fine-tuning teaches style and skills well and facts poorly3: recall
of specific passages is unreliable, updates require retraining, and there's no citation trail.
The strong pattern is both: fine-tune for domain behavior, retrieve for domain
facts. For a corpus that changes daily, retrieval is the only option that stays
current by updating an index.



Is the hashed bag-of-words embedder just a toy?


It's the real hashing trick (scikit-learn ships it as
HashingVectorizer) with a real limitation the lab measures instead of hiding9.
Everything downstream of embed() (index, retrieval, eval) is exactly what runs
under a learned model. That's the point of the design: your project swaps one function and
keeps the pipeline.



What should our project's eval set look like?


10–20 questions a real user of your corpus would ask, each labeled
with the doc(s) that answer it, written by your team, before you tune anything. Grade hit@k
and MRR on every change (chunk size, k, embedding model, hybrid on/off). The proposal rubric
asks for exactly this because it is the difference between measured engineering and guesswork.
