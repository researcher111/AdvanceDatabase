Transactions & the Write-Ahead Log

What COMMIT actually promises, and the append-only file that makes the promise keepable.
Daniel G. Graham · UVA School of Data Science

Way back in Lab 1 you measured how much a durable write costs; today that number becomes the design constraint of the course. A transaction promises all-or-nothing, even through a power cut, while the buffer pool flushes pages whenever it likes. The reconciliation is one of the great tricks in systems: log the old value before every change, in an append-only file, and every promise becomes keepable with a single well-placed fsync. Next Thursday you build it and kill -9 your own engine to prove it.

What COMMIT promises

Think · Pair · Share · the interrupted transfer
  • Think · 2 minA transfer moves $40 from account A to B: subtract, then add: two writes, possibly on two different pages. List every moment a crash could strike, and what the on-disk balances look like at each. Which outcomes are merely annoying, and which one is unacceptable?
I've done the Think step. Reveal Pair & Share
  • Pair · 3 minCompare: crash before = fine (nothing happened); crash after = fine (all happened); crash between = money destroyed or minted. What single capability turns "between" into "before"?
  • Share · 2 minOne sentence: the promise a database must make about multi-write operations.
Show the answer

Three moments. Before either write reaches disk: nothing happened, A and B are untouched, and the transfer simply did not occur. Annoying, correct. After both: the transfer happened in full. Correct. Between the two writes: A has been debited $40 and B never credited, so $40 has been destroyed, and on the reverse ordering it would have been minted. That is the unacceptable one, and no amount of care in the application can prevent it, because the crash lands between two instructions.

The capability that turns "between" into "before" is the ability to undo: if the engine can put A back the way it was, the half-done transfer becomes the same as the never-started one. And to undo after a crash you must have written the old value somewhere that survives the crash, which is the log. So the promise, in one sentence: a multi-write operation happens entirely or not at all, whatever the moment of failure. That is the A in ACID.

The write-ahead rule

Think · Pair · Share · design the insurance
  • Think · 2 minYou must be able to UNDO any page write after a crash, but the crash erases everything not yet on disk. Design the minimal insurance: what must be written, where, and (the crucial part) in what order relative to the page write it insures? What goes wrong with the opposite order?
I've done the Think step. Reveal Pair & Share
  • Pair · 3 minRun the bad order: page write hits disk, crash, no log record; the old value is gone from the universe. Nothing can undo it. Agree: the log entry must exist first, always.
  • Share · 2 minName the rule. (It's the lecture title.)
Show the answer

Write the old value of whatever you are about to change, to an append-only file, before the page write it insures. One small record per change: which transaction, which file, which block, which offset, and what was there before.

Run the opposite order and the failure is total. The page write reaches disk, the crash arrives, and the log record was never written: the old value now exists nowhere in the universe, so nothing can undo it. The insurance has to be bought before the risk is taken, not after. That is the rule, and it is the lecture's title: write-ahead logging. The log is append-only because sequential writes are the one thing disks do at full speed, which is why logging every change costs far less than it sounds.

The rule, and the record

WRITE-AHEAD:   log(old value)  happens-before  page write  happens-before  page flush

one record per change:   {"kind": "SET_INT", "tx": 2, "file": "acct.tbl",
                          "blk": 0, "off": 0, "old": 100}

the log itself:  an APPEND-ONLY file, no seeks, no slots, no structure.
                 Sequential writes are the one thing disks do at full speed,
                 which is why logging every change costs less than it sounds.

The log stores old values because our recovery only ever undoes. (ARIES logs new values too, so it can also redo; same rule, more insurance.)

Where the fsyncs go, and don't

Eventfsync?Why
each SET recordno, buffered appendthe record must merely be ahead of its page; pages flush rarely
data pages at commityes (our FORCE policy)data on disk before the commit record exists
the COMMIT recordyes, THE fsyncwhen it returns, the promise is binding; before it, the tx never happened
ROLLBACK recordsat leisurea lost rollback record just means recovery re-undoes, harmless (idempotence)

Lab 1's ratio, resolved: a transaction touching fifty pages pays one mandatory fsync, on a sequential file. That arithmetic is why databases can promise durability and still be fast.

War story · "fsyncgate," 2018

If one fsync carries the entire durability promise, its error handling had better be perfect, and for twenty years, Postgres's wasn't. In 2018 the community discovered that on Linux, when an fsync fails with an I/O error, the kernel can mark the dirty pages clean anyway; retrying the fsync then returns success while the data never reached disk. Postgres had assumed retry-until-success was safe. The fix, shipped urgently across every supported version, was brutal honesty: if THE fsync fails, crash the whole server and run recovery from the WAL, because the log is now the only copy you can trust. Search "fsyncgate" for the full thread; it is the best systems reading of that year, and it is exactly this slide's table with real stakes.

Recovery: undo the unfinished

A crash leaves the disk mid-sentence: committed work possibly complete, unfinished work possibly half-flushed (stolen pages!). Recovery's contract is modest and absolute: make the disk look as if every unfinished transaction never began. One backward pass over the log does it.

Found on disk after the crashWhy it's thereRecovery's move
committed dataFORCE flushed it before its COMMIT recordkeep; its fate says so
uncommitted dataa stolen dirty pageundo; the log holds the old value
missing uncommitted datanever flushed; the crash ate the buffernothing to do; already gone

One backward pass

for rec in log.records_backwards():
if rec.kind in (COMMIT, ROLLBACK): finished.add(rec.tx)
elif rec.kind is SET and rec.tx not in finished:
restore(rec.file, rec.blk, rec.off, rec.old)
log ROLLBACK for each undone tx; flush
Hover a line: recovery is rollback for transactions that can no longer speak for themselves.

Crash during recovery?

Then recovery runs again at the next startup, and must do no new damage. Undo-only recovery is naturally idempotent: restoring an old value twice restores it once. The receipt records make the second pass skip cleanly; the lab tests literally run recover() twice and check nothing moves. Systems design rule worth framing: any repair process must survive being interrupted by the disaster it repairs.

Force, steal, and ARIES

Think · Pair · Share · the 2×2, cashed in
  • Think · 2 minWeek 2's grid: FORCE = flush data pages at commit; STEAL = the pool may flush uncommitted pages anytime. For each quadrant, decide: does recovery need UNDO? REDO? Both? Neither? (Reason from what could be on disk at crash time.)
I've done the Think step. Reveal Pair & Share
  • Pair · 3 minCheck: steal puts uncommitted data on disk → undo needed. No-force leaves committed data off disk → redo needed. microdb picks force+steal: undo only.
  • Share · 2 minWhy does every real engine pick the quadrant needing BOTH?
Show the answer

Reason from what the disk can hold at the moment of the crash. Steal lets uncommitted data reach disk, so recovery needs undo. No-force lets committed data still be missing from disk, so recovery needs redo. That gives the grid: no-steal + force needs neither, no-steal + no-force needs redo, steal + force needs undo (microdb's corner), and steal + no-force needs both.

Every real engine picks the both corner, because the other three buy their simple recovery by crippling normal operation. Force means every commit waits for scattered random page writes, so commit latency is hostage to the data pages rather than to one sequential log write. No-steal means the buffer pool may not evict a dirty page belonging to a live transaction, so a long transaction can pin the pool solid. Steal plus no-force gives the pool complete freedom and makes commit cost exactly one sequential fsync; the price is a recovery algorithm with two passes, which is a price you pay only after a crash.

FAQ

If the log is so critical, what happens when IT gets corrupted?

Real WALs checksum every record and treat the first bad checksum as end-of-log; a torn final record just means that transaction never committed (its fsync can't have returned). The log's append-only shape is what makes this safe: corruption can only live at the tail.

Doesn't logging every change double the write traffic?

Roughly, but the log's writes are sequential appends (the fast kind, Lab 1) while the pages they protect are random writes that can now be deferred and batched. Net effect on real hardware: WAL made databases faster, which is the punchline nobody expects.

What's a checkpoint actually for, then?

Bounding recovery time. Without one, a year-old database replays a year of log; a checkpoint records "everything before here is on disk," so recovery starts there. Week 2's table said "flush at checkpoints"; this is the debt that line was foreshadowing.

Does SQLite have a WAL? My phone doesn't feel like a bank.

It does (PRAGMA journal_mode=WAL), and your phone uses it constantly; every messaging app is a little bank of messages that must not vanish. The other SQLite mode (rollback journal) is the same insurance with the copies made before changes instead of beside them.
