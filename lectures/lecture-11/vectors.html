Vector Databases

When the column is an embedding and the query is "what's similar?": indexes for a world without equality.
Daniel G. Graham · UVA School of Data Science

You already trust embeddings, vectors whose geometry encodes meaning. The database question they raise is new: every index you've met accelerates equality and order (uid = 7, gpa > 35), but "the 10 chunks most similar to this question" has no equality anywhere. B+ trees are useless here; hashing is worse. The answer is a new index family (approximate nearest neighbor) that buys speed with a currency you've never spent before: correctness, measured and dialed. Thursday you build IVF and set the dial yourself; your project's retrieval layer will sit on it.

Companion reading: pgvector's README (the whole design in one page) · the HNSW paper: Malkov & Yashunin, Efficient and robust ANN search using Hierarchical Navigable Small World graphs · FAISS's index-choosing guide.

The similarity query

The setup, precisely

store:   n vectors, d dims (n = millions, d = 384-3072 for real embedding models)
query:   top-k by similarity, cosine for unit vectors, which is just the dot product:

    sim(q, v) = q · v = Σ qᵢvᵢ          (after normalizing: divide by length once, at insert)

exact answer:  n dot products per query = n·d multiplications.
             1M vectors × 1536 dims × every user question = the wall.

Exact search and its wall

Before approximating anything, price the exact thing: score all n vectors, keep the top k. Correct by construction, embarrassingly parallel, and linear in everything: the baseline that never lies and eventually never finishes.

What brute force costs, and when it's fine

ScaleExact cost per queryVerdict
4k × 32-d (Thursday's lab)128k mults, sub-millisecondfine; measure it anyway
100k × 768-d (a real project corpus)77M mults, tens of msfine-ish, single user
10M × 1536-d, 100 qps (production RAG)1.5 × 10¹² mults/secthe wall

Honest advice your project should hear: at small scale, brute force is the right answer: exact, simple, zero recall anxiety. The project spec requires the brute-force baseline for exactly this reason. Indexes earn their keep at scale, like always.

ANN: IVF and HNSW

IVF: cluster, then probe

build:   k-means into C lists (C ≈ √n is folklore)      once, offline
search:  score C centroids → take the best P ("probe")  → brute-force inside those lists

cost ≈ C + P·(n/C) instead of n:   4,000 vectors, C=20, P=4  →  ~880 vs 4,000  (4.5×)
                                   1M vectors, C=1000, P=10  →  ~11k vs 1M     (90×)

the miss: a true neighbor in an unprobed list. P is the dial.

HNSW: navigate, don't partition

HNSW's greedy walk · highways to get close, streets to finish

Twelve vectors, two layers. The top layer keeps a few nodes with long-range links (the highways); the bottom layer links every node to its near neighbors (the streets). Step the walk: from the entry point, always hop to whichever neighbor is closest to the query ★; when no neighbor improves, drop a layer and keep walking.

The walk reaches the true nearest neighbor in a handful of hops, computing distances only along the path; that near-logarithmic hop count is why HNSW usually wins the recall-at-speed benchmarks. Its dial is ef_search, the width of the frontier the walk keeps: same contract as IVF's probe, wider = better recall, more work.

The dialef_search: how wide a frontier the walk keeps. Same trade as probe: bigger = better recall, more work.
The pricethe graph lives in RAM and inserts are costlier than IVF's; the default when recall-at-speed matters most, IVF when memory or build time bind.

Recall: the honesty metric

recall@k  =  |approximate top-k  ∩  true top-k|  /  k

  1.0  = found everything the exact search would have
  0.75 = missed a quarter of the real neighbors - and told you so

Read it like an engineer: at probe 1 you're wrong about 1 in 4 neighbors and 17× faster; at probe 4 you're wrong about 1 in 20 and still 4.5× faster. Neither is "right": a recommender shrugs at 0.76; retrieved evidence for a RAG answer should not. The dial is yours, and "what recall does this application need?" is now a question you ask out loud.

Check yourself · score an index by hand

Exact search says the true top-10 for a query is ids {1…10}. Your IVF at probe 2 returns, in order: 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 11, 14, 20. Compute recall@10, and then explain where ids 4, 7, and 10 must be, physically, for this result to have happened.

Show answer

Seven of the true ten appear ({1,2,3,5,6,8,9}), so recall@10 = 0.7. Ids 4, 7, and 10 sit in cluster lists the two probes didn't visit; they were never compared, not out-scored; 11, 14, 20 filled their seats. Raising probe can only add candidate lists, so recall can only rise, which is exactly the monotonicity Thursday's harness tests, and the reason a "probe more, recall drops" bug always means the probe isn't selecting what you think.

The landscape

Every product in the vector-database gold rush is some packaging of the two structures you just met, plus the dial. What varies is where it lives relative to the rest of your data:

Three ways to ship a vector index

ShapeExamplesWhen
Inside your databasepgvector (Postgres), DuckDB's VSSvectors next to their rows: joins, filters, transactions included; the boring, usually-right default
A libraryFAISS, hnswlibyou own the serving code; maximal control and speed, batteries not included
A dedicated servicePinecone, Weaviate, Milvus, Qdrantmanaged scale and ops, plus a second copy of your data to keep in sync (see FAQ)

For the project: microvector or pgvector or FAISS all satisfy the spec; what's graded is that your retrieval layer is a real index and you can defend its dial setting with your own measurements.

FAQ

Why cosine instead of Euclidean distance?

On unit vectors they induce the same ranking (‖q−v‖² = 2−2q·v), so the argument is moot once you normalize, which embedding models mostly assume. Cosine's virtue is invariance to length, which for embeddings encodes "topic, not intensity."

Is recall the only quality metric that matters?

It's the index's metric: did the structure find the true geometric neighbors? Whether those neighbors are useful is the embedding model's burden and the pipeline's (next lecture: end-to-end evaluation, where retrieval hit-rate and answer quality get separated on purpose).

The dedicated vector databases advertise hard: when do they earn it?

When vectors are your primary workload at serious scale: billions of vectors (usually quantized), filtered ANN, multi-tenant serving. Below that, a second datastore means a second copy of truth, sync jobs, and consistency questions you now know by name. The boring answer (vectors in the database you already run) wins more often than the marketing implies.

Could microdb have a vector index?

Structurally yes: IVF lists are just tables (centroid_id, RID), and Lab 6's IndexSelectScan pattern applies verbatim. That is, in fact, roughly what pgvector is to Postgres: new index type, same RID handshake. Nice final-project appendix for the microdb-attached.
