Build a RAG pipeline

Advanced Databases for Data Science · Fall 2026 · proposal Oct 29 → presentation Dec 8

Retrieval-augmented generation is the most database-shaped problem in modern machine learning: a corpus to ingest, an index to build, a query path to make fast, and a results quality bar to measure honestly. Your team will build a complete RAG system in Python over a corpus you choose, and because you built a vector index with your own hands in Lab 9, yours will not be a black box.

Design your tables before you write code

The most common way a data scientist's RAG project fails is not a bad embedding model or a slow index. It is that documents, chunks, embeddings, and evaluation questions live in four ad-hoc Python lists and dicts that drift apart. The chunk list gets rebuilt with a new chunk size, the embedding array does not, and the evaluation questions still point at list positions that no longer mean anything. Every number in the report is then a number about a system that no longer exists.

So design the tables first, before any pipeline code, the way Lab 3 made you write the catalog before a table scan could read a row. Four tables are enough:

TableColumnsKeys
documents doc_id, source, title, ingested_at, plus any metadata your corpus carries (author, date, URL) Primary key doc_id. One row per source document, written once at ingestion and never rewritten.
chunks chunk_id, doc_id, ordinal, text, n_tokens Primary key chunk_id. doc_id is a foreign key to documents; ordinal is the chunk's position within its document.
embeddings chunk_id, model, dim, vector Primary key (chunk_id, model); chunk_id references chunks. If your index stores its own vectors (FAISS, your Lab 9 microvector), drop the vector column and keep the row as a record that the vector exists in the index keyed by chunk_id.
eval_questions q_id, question, relevant_doc_ids, author Primary key q_id. Every id in relevant_doc_ids is a doc_id in documents; author is the team member who read the source and wrote the question.

One foreign key matters more than the rest: chunks.doc_id to documents.doc_id. It is what lets a retrieved chunk always name its source, so every answer your system generates can cite a document, not just a chunk number. The demo requirement below (an answer with cited chunks) depends on this join working.

The other choices exist for the evaluation. Chunk ids must be stable across runs: assign chunk_id from (doc_id, ordinal) or from a hash of the chunk text, never from a list position, so the retrieval numbers you report on Nov 12 can be reproduced on Dec 3. The model column on embeddings is what lets you compare two embedders on the same chunks, which is the cheapest of the two-configuration comparisons the Requirements ask for. And relevant_doc_ids is labeled at the document level, not the chunk level, so that re-chunking the corpus (which you will do, because chunk size is the first thing worth tuning) does not invalidate a single label.

This is the same discipline Lab 10 applied to micro-rag: its five-stage pipeline table names what each stage takes in and hands off before any stage is written. Your four tables do that for the data instead of the code. Your proposal must include them, with keys, and the retrieval milestone will be checked against them.

Requirements

  • Real corpus, your choice. At least ~500 documents or ~2,000 chunks after splitting. Ideas: UVA course catalogs and policy pages, a Wikipedia category dump, arXiv abstracts in your field, cooking recipes, court opinions, game rulebooks, your favorite podcast's transcripts. Must be legally shareable with the teaching staff.
  • Python, with the database doing real work. The retrieval layer must be a genuine index (your microvector from Lab 9, pgvector, FAISS, or DuckDB's vector extension), not a linear scan hidden in a loop (a brute-force baseline is required for comparison, but it can't be the product).
  • An evaluation you can defend. Author ≥ 25 evaluation questions with known source passages. Report retrieval quality (recall@k and MRR against those passages) and answer quality (graded rubric or LLM-judge with spot checks). Compare at least two configurations (e.g. chunk size, k, brute-force vs ANN, with vs without reranking) and say which won and why.
  • Measured performance. Index build time, queries per second, and the recall-vs-speed tradeoff your ANN settings chose.
  • A demo. Live at the presentation: a question we ask, an answer with cited chunks, in seconds.
Stretch goals (pick at most one, only if the core is solid) Hybrid retrieval (BM25 + vector with score fusion) · GraphRAG over a Kuzu graph of your corpus's entities · multi-hop questions · a text-to-SQL agent over a relational slice of your data · incremental ingestion (new documents indexed without a rebuild).

The question swap exists because questions written by people who did not build the system are the only ones that cannot be tuned to. Your own 25 questions shape every choice you make about chunk size, k, and reranking, so by the workshop your system is fitted to them whether you intended that or not. The partner team's 10 questions are this course's version of a held-out test set: the gap between your numbers on their questions and on your own is the most honest measurement in your report.

Ground rules

  • Teams of 2–3. The report includes a one-paragraph contribution statement per member.
  • LLM access: use the course-provided endpoint (details in class) or any model you can run locally; do not pay for API access to do this project.
  • AI assistance: allowed for code, with attribution, but your team must be able to explain every line at the presentation, and the evaluation questions must be authored by humans who read the corpus.
  • Off-the-shelf frameworks (LangChain, LlamaIndex) are allowed only for the generation step. Chunking, indexing, and retrieval must be code your team wrote. That's the point of the course.
